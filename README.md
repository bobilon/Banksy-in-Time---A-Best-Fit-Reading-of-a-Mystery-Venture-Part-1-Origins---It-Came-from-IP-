I. The Buffet
By the summer of 1998, the most valuable unclaimed asset class in transatlantic youth culture was a forty-year photographic archive of crimes nobody had been paid for.
The crimes were minor and the archive was enormous. Larry Clark had been shooting Tulsa junkies since 1971 and had moved into the Manhattan skate scene by the time the Kids script went around in 1994 — by which point his back catalogue was already understood inside the New York gallery system as a documentary-photography asset class with established auction comparables. Martha Cooper and Henry Chalfant had inventoried the entire first generation of New York subway writers in Subway Art (Thames & Hudson, 1984) — a book that by the late nineties was passing hand-to-hand through every art school in Britain like contraband scripture, and whose continuous in-print status from 1984 forward made it the single most distributed visual document of American graffiti practice in European circulation. The Mod photographers who had keynoted the visual world of Quadrophenia (1979) had aged into the same dealer rooms as the punk documentarians who'd shot the Sex Pistols for Vivienne Westwood and Malcolm McLaren — a promotional apparatus McLaren himself, fluent in Marshall McLuhan and unembarrassed about it, understood as a product launch the entire time. He had said so in print. He had said so on television. The Pistols were a McLuhan thesis with a guitar player.
Jean-Michel Basquiat had completed the laundering proof-of-concept by his 1982 Annina Nosei show and his 1983 inclusion in the Whitney Biennial; Keith Haring had run the same trajectory through Tony Shafrazi by 1982. Both were dead by 1990. Their secondary-market trajectories were visible data by 1998 to anyone reading the trade press: street mark to gallery wall to Sotheby's, with the auction record compounding for a decade after the artist's death because the supply was now fixed. Charles Saatchi had been running the same play one register up through the Young British Artists since the late eighties. Damien Hirst's The Physical Impossibility of Death in the Mind of Someone Living — the shark — had cleared in 1991. Sensation had opened at the Royal Academy in September 1997, eleven months before Walls on Fire. Anyone in the British publishing class who could read a calendar could see what the next regional play looked like.
RUN-DMC had been on MTV since 1986, which meant that the cross-pollination Greenwich Village jazz clubs had been running quietly since the fifties — Black musical labor crossing into white cultural prestige economies through nightclub geography — was now a continuous broadcast signal, fully populated across every social register a buyer might occupy. My Generation had been on the shelf for thirty-three years. Blank Generation for twenty-one. Raising Hell for twelve. The thesis that defiance was the most reliable consumer product in postwar Anglo-American culture was no longer a thesis. It was a P&L statement covering four decades.
What this produced, by 1998, was not a culture. It was a buffet — a horizontally integrated menu of salable transatlantic IP whose creators, in nearly every case, had been paid in scene credibility rather than equity. The photographs existed. The negatives sat in flat files. The graphic backings — the tags, the throw-ups, the hand-painted show flyers, the zine artwork, the club photography — were documented in thousands of frames held by photographers who, at the rates then prevailing in the British music and style press, would license a print run for the cost of a month's rent in a Hackney squat.
II. The Horizontal Read
The producers and rights aggregators inside fashion, music, film, television, and advertising were not, by 1997, in distinct businesses. They were in the same business, and they knew it.
The consumer experience was sectoral — you bought a record, you bought a shirt, you watched a film, you saw an ad — but the upstream operators were running rights aggregation against a single underlying asset class: youth-culture feedstock with a documented forty-year compounding curve. The window in which that feedstock could be acquired at pre-platform prices was visibly closing. The World Wide Web had been in public use since 1993. Amazon had launched in 1994, eBay in 1995, Google had incorporated in September 1998 — the same month Pro-Actiff Communications was being prepared for filing in London. Anyone with a working understanding of distribution costs could see that the analogue-to-digital transition was about to collapse the marginal cost of global content distribution to approximately zero, and that whoever held the rights to the standing analogue archive at the moment of collapse would own a cross-border, cross-sector IP position whose addressable market was the entire wired population of the planet.
The instances of this play that the reader of 2026 already accepts as historical fact are the proof of the pattern.
Supreme had opened on Lafayette Street in April 1994, selling a visual logic borrowed wholesale from Barbara Kruger — the red box, the white Futura Heavy Oblique — back to Manhattan skaters for T-shirt money. Kruger's own work had been a critique of advertising's appropriation of desire; Supreme inverted the critique into the product. In 2017, the Carlyle Group bought a fifty-percent stake at a $1 billion valuation. In 2020, VF Corporation acquired the company for $2.1 billion. The starting capital had been a downtown lease and a printer.
Nike's Air Jordan line had launched in 1985 against Michael Jordan's rookie NBA contract; by 1997 the Jordan Brand had been spun out as a Nike subsidiary and was running its own multi-billion-dollar P&L, with Spike Lee's Mars Blackmon advertising sequence (1988–1991) having established that the athlete-as-IP could compound across apparel, advertising, film, and standalone brand architecture. By 1997 the play was a documented case taught in business schools. Jordan is named here as touchpoint rather than as full instance — its 1985–1997 trajectory predates the specific 1994–2001 incorporation window this part documents, but its commercial outcome (Jordan Brand's standalone multi-billion P&L) is the visible proof that the mechanism had been demonstrated to work at scale before any of the operations Pro-Actiff would learn from were incorporated.
KAWS — Brian Donnelly — is the cleanest fashion-and-fine-art-sector instance because the mechanism operates without occlusion. Donnelly was a suburban art-school graduate working as an animator on the Nickelodeon series Doug (Jim Jinkins Productions) through 2002, with a parallel street practice based in tag-and-name interventions on bus-shelter and phone-booth advertising whose documentary record in Gregory J. Snyder's Graffiti Lives: Beyond the Tag in New York's Urban Underground (NYU Press, 2009) places him in the New York scene from the late nineties without claiming significant artistic-production substance for the period. Snyder's reportorial basis for Donnelly's inclusion is the names-and-tags evidence, which is the basis the analogue documentary tradition is equipped to verify. The fine-art production that the KAWS brand would later sell at Phillips and Sotheby's as autonomous artist work originates, by Donnelly's own public account, in the 2003–2006 commission relationship with Nigo (Tomoaki Nagao, founder of A Bathing Ape) and Takashi Murakami — a period during which the painted output was produced as commissioned brand-and-character work for the Asian streetwear and contemporary-art infrastructures those two principals operated.
The mechanism is worth naming precisely because it is not fronted-name-over-hidden-equity. It is a different and arguably more sophisticated instance of the same terminal effect on the working-artist economy, and Donnelly's transparency about the structure is the analytical key that distinguishes it.
Donnelly is the smart fanboy who became a brand manager and an art entrepreneur — a role with a direct precedent in Damien Hirst's parallel position as artist-collector, but operating without Hirst's megalomaniacal self-presentation and with a structurally different relationship to the underlying production. The KAWS brand was built through Asian vinyl-figure manufacturing and retail infrastructure — Medicom Toy in Japan, All Rights Reserved in Hong Kong — that, from the outset, cut Donnelly into the IP economics of the goods being manufactured rather than positioning him as the singular artistic author whose hand produced them. This is the inverse of the Banksy structure and the inverse of the traditional fine-art-attribution structure both: the brand was the asset, the manufacturing-and-retail infrastructure was the production base, and Donnelly's role was the brand-management-and-character-development role that the consumer products and luxury-goods industries had been compensating at executive scale for decades. Murakami's contemporaneous work — including the 2007 MOCA-originated © MURAKAMI exhibition cycle and the Louis Vuitton collaboration that brought Asian commercial-art-brand aesthetics into the LVMH luxury infrastructure — addressed directly the post-war colonization of Asian visual culture by Disney and the broader American consumer-products industry, and turned the defacement-and-reappropriation of those figures into a saleable brand position. KAWS, operating in parallel, brought the same Asian commercial-art-brand value into the same luxury infrastructure through the same channels.
The reason this matters for the Banksy reading is that it documents a second working model by which the standing transatlantic IP feedstock could be commercialized inside the 1994–2001 window: not by manufacturing a fronted singular-artist legend, but by transparently operating as a brand manager whose product is character-and-graphic IP and whose distribution rail is consumer-products manufacturing rather than fine-art editioning. Both models terminate in the same effect — the gallery and auction systems absorbing brand-managed product as fine art on trust-the-promoters grounds, with consequences for the working-artist economy that the analogue documentary tradition would have made visible in real time and the consolidated-media environment of the late nineties did not. The Banksy operation chose the fronted-singular-artist legend. The KAWS operation chose the transparent-brand-manager structure. Both produced nine-figure-plus commercial outcomes inside the same window, which is the evidence that the sectoral mechanism — IP capture against standing youth-culture feedstock at pre-platform prices — was the actual operative variable, and the choice between fronted-legend and transparent-brand-management was downstream marketing strategy rather than upstream structural difference.
The forensic point is not that KAWS is suspicious. KAWS is, by Donnelly's own public account and by the documentary record of the Nigo and Murakami relationships, exactly what it presents as: a brand-management-and-art-entrepreneurship operation that absorbed gallery-system fine-art validation as one of several distribution channels for its character IP. The forensic point is that the gallery system absorbed it on those terms — that the trust-the-promoters convention, which had emerged inside the consolidated-media environment of the 1990s as the verification vacuum left behind by the analogue press's institutional collapse, was sufficient to convert transparently brand-managed character IP into auction-validated fine art without the documentary friction the analogue tradition would have generated. Once that conversion was demonstrated to work, the singular-artist legend became optional as a marketing strategy rather than necessary as a structural requirement — which is the standing condition the Banksy operation chose to operate inside, and which the next sections will document as the commercial environment Pro-Actiff was incorporated to exploit.
Gorillaz is the cleanest adjacent instance inside the immediate Pro-Actiff window because it lands inside the same eighteen-month incubation period. Justine Frischmann had left Damon Albarn in mid-1997, ending the relationship that had defined Britpop's gendered marketing apparatus for half a decade. Albarn's collaboration with Jamie Hewlett on what would become Gorillaz began incubating immediately. Blur was a regional pop brand that had failed to break globally — the 1997 American radio breakthrough on "Song 2" was an outlier inside an album sequence whose UK-vs-US commercial performance was incommensurate. Tank Girl, Hewlett's prior IP, had flopped in its 1995 film adaptation but retained underground equity. The Gorillaz solution — a virtual band whose visible IP holders were Albarn and Hewlett, fronting a structure whose actual creative attribution was occluded by design — launched its first album in 2001 and produced a multi-album, cross-platform commercial property whose financial trajectory, by 2026, sits in the high nine figures. Hewlett's documented animation practice, in which characters do not age and do not change fashion across episodes, sits in observable tension with the Noodle character's evolving fashion imprint — a discontinuity that the Lucy McKenzie material, held for later parts of this series, will address at full evidentiary weight. For Part 1's purposes, what matters is that the music-sector instance of the same play was incubating in London inside the same calendar year as Pro-Actiff's incorporation, with structural homology too close to be coincidence and personnel networks that would prove to overlap.
The reader does not need to accept the Banksy thesis on its own merits. The reader needs to recognize that the Banksy thesis is structurally identical to commercial outcomes already accepted as historical fact across four adjacent sectors, all incubating inside the same 1994–2001 window, all responding to the same standing condition: the analogue archive was about to become globally addressable, and the rights had not yet been priced.
III. The Labor Pool Was the Silent Variable
The variable the standard Banksy story refuses to name is the one that makes everything that follows rational rather than miraculous.
The late-nineties art-school graduate and the derelict-scene participant occupied the same economic position. The twentieth-century social contract — that formal credentials would convert to lifetime employment by profession — had been visibly defaulting on the British cohort that came of age between 1989 and 1998. The Thatcher restructuring had foreclosed the public-sector and heavy-industry pathways their parents had occupied; the financial-services expansion had absorbed a small London-centric fraction of the credential-holding population while leaving the regional and creative-credentialed remainder to the gig economy of zine work, club flyers, scene photography, music journalism, freelance graphic design, and protest-art documentation. This was not a bohemian choice. It was the only working revenue stream available to a cohort whose formal credentials had been retroactively devalued by macroeconomic conditions they did not choose and could not influence.
The rates the British style press paid for scene photography in 1997–1998 were, in real terms, a fraction of what the same magazines had paid a decade earlier. Dazed & Confused, which Jefferson Hack had launched with Rankin Waddell and Ian Taylor in 1991, had moved by the late nineties from its founding bootstrap economy into a more professionalized operation, with Hack stepping back from daily editorial in 1998 — a graduation move whose timing aligns precisely with the Pro-Actiff incorporation. Sleazenation, which had launched in 1996, was running on a freelance budget that paid scene photographers in copies and credits as often as in cash. Juxtapoz, founded by Robert Williams in 1994 as the West Coast lowbrow-art counterpart, was running a similar economy through a different sectoral door. The German-American Gingko Press operation that distributed Juxtapoz internationally and would publish Shepard Fairey's Supply and Demand(2006) — and a decade later Banksy's You Are an Acceptable Level of Threat (2012, not authored by "Banksy" — a forensic point held for later parts) — was the through-line that connected the West Coast lowbrow lane to the European publishing rail.
The PYMCA archive — the Photographic Youth Music Culture Archive, founded by Nicky Browne in 1996 and registered as a not-for-profit at Old Street, London EC2A 4HJ — functioned as the image aggregator for this entire labor pool. PYMCA's house photographers and contributing archivists included, by the early 2000s, Steve Lazarides, who would also author Skinz n Punkz (PYMCA, 2001) on the British black music scene; Robin Gunningham, documented as a contributing photographer in the same period; and a working roster of scene photographers whose primary income came from licensing their archive into the publications Hack and his counterparts were producing. The labor pool was cheap. It was networked. It was already producing the work product. And it had no leverage to demand IP terms, because the work product was technically evidence of a misdemeanor.
This is the standing condition into which the Walls on Fire announcement landed in August 1998.
IV. Bristol Was the Last Unclaimed Node
The Walls on Fire event itself was, in commercial terms, unremarkable — a regional mural showcase of a kind that had been running in Philadelphia since the 1984 Mural Arts Program, in New York since the early eighties through the Fashion Moda and PS1 axis, in Berlin since the post-Wall reorganization of the eastern districts, in São Paulo since the pixação movement of the late seventies. What was remarkable was that Bristol, by 1998, was the last unclaimed regional node in the network whose monetization protocol had already been demonstrated everywhere else.
New York had been wrapped by the Cooper-Chalfant axis and its gallery downstream through Patti Astor's Fun Gallery and the Shafrazi operation. Los Angeles had been wrapped by the Beautiful Losers cohort moving toward what would become Juxtapoz's house style. Berlin had its own documentary apparatus through the post-Wall photography scene. Tokyo had Nigo and the Ura-Harajuku axis. Bristol — with a deep bench of writers including 3D (Robert Del Naja), Inkie (Tom Bingle), Nick Walker, and the Wild Bunch / Massive Attack infrastructure that had already proven the city's regional-identity-reads-as-authentic export pattern through the music industry between 1991 and 1998 — was inventory waiting for a distributor. The music industry had run this play in Bristol five years earlier with trip-hop. Pro-Actiff was preparing to run it again with a different product class.
The criminality-as-feedstock variable was, in Bristol specifically, at its most legible. Operation Anderson of 1989 — the British Transport Police's coordinated action against London Underground writers — had charged eighty-plus individuals with criminal damage, with sentences ranging from community service to custodial terms. The arc from 1989 prosecutions to 1998 municipal mural permitting was nine years. The same act, by the same demographic, in the same legal jurisdiction, had moved from prosecutable misdemeanor to event programming inside a single decade. The commercial thesis stated in legal-historical terms was: the criminal record of the act is the authentication of the product; the legalization of the venue is the monetization of the authentication. You could not have built this. You only had to recognize that it had built itself, and incorporate before anyone else did.
The Walls on Fire announcement, posted in early August 1998 on flyers and event listings circulated through the Bristol music venues and the Sleazenation distribution network, was signed by two writers above their typed crew designations: Inkie XXX and Banks DBS.

Read the announcement.
The signature is not "Banksy." It is "Banks DBS" — Banks of the DBS crew, signing as a member of one of the Bristol crews the document itself names ("Crews so far confirmed include DBS, FSH, SOF, SINSTARS and ILC"). The typed caption beneath the hand-drawn signature confirms the attribution: Banks DBS. Not a stylization of "Banksy." Not a y-suffix variant. A crew member operating under a different name, with a different identity construction, three months before the corporate vehicle that would incorporate the "Banksy" brand was filed at Companies House.
The single-letter shift from Banks to Banksy is the forensic hinge on which the entire legend turns, and the documentary evidence is the flyer itself. There was no "Banksy" at the lead-up to Walls on Fire. There was a writer named Banks operating within the DBS crew at a regional graffiti championship, signing event correspondence in his crew capacity. The components of the legend that would later be filled in — the Bristol origin, the working-class biography, the early stencil practice, the formative Barton Hill youth-club period — are likely true of someone in the mix. The mix was deep. Bristol in 1998 contained dozens of artists, fabricators, commercial production workers, music-scene functionaries, art-school dropouts, and derelict-scene participants whose individual biographies would, in some combination, populate the elements of the eventual public legend. What is foreclosed by the document itself is the claim that those elements describe a single individual operating under the brand name "Banksy" before the autumn of 1998.
The shift from Banks to Banksy is the move from a writer's tag to a brand. In Bristol scene shorthand, Banks was a person operating within a crew. The y-suffix is the conversion to character — the move from underground writer to publishable commercial identity, the move that takes a tag and makes it a trademark. The conversion was performed by the corporate vehicle that incorporated to perform it.
Three months after the show, on 15 October 1998, in London, the corporate vehicle was filed.
VI. The Document
Pro-Actiff Communications Limited, Companies House registration number 03650422, registered under SIC code 58190 — Other publishing activities — went on the register on 15 October 1998 under the original filing name Identity Crisis Limited. The Certificate of Incorporation on Change of Name records the rebrand to Pro-Actiff Communications Limited on 11 December 1998 — a fifty-seven-day window during which the corporate vehicle operated under the working-title designation its founders had filed it under.
The two-month gap is a forensic datum. The founders did not pre-plan the Pro-Actiff name and file under it. They filed under Identity Crisis, operated under that name for almost two months, and rebranded to the public-facing designation in mid-December — which means Identity Crisis was the founders' working-title description of what the entity did, retained long enough for it to appear on the corporate record before being swapped for a name that did not telegraph the function. A founder names a company what they think the company does. Then, eight weeks later, when it becomes time to operate the company in public, they rename it something that does not say what it does. The original name is the founders' private statement of intent. The renaming is the operational decision to obscure that intent for public consumption. Both are now permanent Companies House record.
The directors of record are Gary Shove and Sharon Shove, with two additional named stakeholders whose registered details warrant the closer Companies House scrutiny that subsequent parts of this series will provide. Pro-Actiff's principal owner has never been publicly stated. The company has been fronted, across its operational history, by the Shove directorship, with the underlying equity unacknowledged in the public record. This is, in itself, a forensic datum: a publishing entity operating at the scale Pro-Actiff would eventually operate at, in the visible center of the British style-press infrastructure, whose ownership has been kept off the public register for the duration of its commercial life is operating that way for reasons.
The inferential case for whom the Shove-fronted structure was incorporated to serve converges, on the available evidence, on Jefferson Hack.
Hack had launched Dazed & Confused in 1991 with Rankin Waddell and Ian Taylor and had spent the intervening seven years watching exactly the feedstock Pro-Actiff was incorporated to capture move through his own pages without anyone holding the upstream rights. He stepped back from Dazed's daily editorial operation in 1998 — the graduation move whose timing aligns precisely with the Pro-Actiff incorporation. The operational evidence assembled by working-class production-side informants confirms that Banksy's launch-and-operational-period publicity ran through Dazed before being released by Jo Brooks, the front-facing publicist, with the upstream coordination running through Suzanne Waddell — the Waddell-Hack nexus to the Banksy operation operating across two distinct points of connection. The Daily Mailattribution piece in 2008 named Hack as Banksy's publisher; Euronews subsequently covered the question and named Hack in the same role. The clearances logic on the 2005 Kate Moss print — the work whose secret-commission discrepancy is now the substance of the 2026 Ant and Dec v. Lilley High Court action — points to Hack as the only principal in the operation with the personal access required to obtain Moss's likeness clearances, with Lily Allen as the daughter of Hack and Moss being the personal anchor that makes the clearance question one of family negotiation rather than commercial licensing. The Steph Warren corroboration documented in James Peak's The Banksy Story — Warren attending a Hack party during the Crude Oils exhibition run, as the only crew-adjacent figure who had not signed an NDA — is the on-the-record account by an inside-position witness that places Banksy's principal at the Hack social infrastructure during the operation's most public London phase.
The convergent inference: Pro-Actiff's owner has never been publicly stated, but the connections that would have been required to wire the operation as it ran — the Dazed publicity rail, the Suzanne Waddell coordination chain, the Jo Brooks public-facing release, the Moss clearances, the Warren corroboration, the personal nexus through Hack's family and party infrastructure, and Hack's silence in the face of sustained and direct public attribution across hashtag campaigns, public DM correspondence, and Euronews coverage naming him in the role — are connections that, taken together, no other principal in the British publishing class of the period possessed. The case is circumstantial in the formal evidentiary sense and as close to foregone in the practical analytical sense as the public record permits, short of a Companies House filing or a confession. Hack's silence is its own datum. The public record's silence on Pro-Actiff's principal ownership is its own datum. The two silences together describe the structure they were both incorporated to maintain.
The corporate vehicle preceded the brand. It preceded the artist. It preceded, by several years, the first object the venture would sell at auction. What it did not precede was the inventory, which had been accumulating on the floor of transatlantic youth culture since before most of the eventual principals were born.
A second corporate vehicle, also wired into what would become the Banksy operation, was incorporated under the original name Underwater Records Limited before being renamed to its eventual designation, GBFTCU — an acronym whose expansion will be addressed in Part 2's treatment of the publishing-rail architecture, where the entity's operational role becomes legible. The original-name choices in both cases — Identity Crisis and Underwater Records — telegraph camouflage in different sectoral idioms: the publishing-and-rights idiom of a vehicle designed to manage a fronted persona, and the music-industry idiom of a vehicle designed to operate beneath the visible surface of its sector. Founders name companies what they think the companies do.
The adjacent corporate stake-out is the Damien Hirst infrastructure: Science Ltd (incorporated 2001, Hirst's primary rights and production vehicle), Other Criteria (incorporated 2005, the publishing and editions arm), and Turtleneck (one of the holding entities in the broader Hirst structure). Hirst's vehicles are named here as the visible benchmark for what an artist-as-corporate-IP-architecture looks like when the artist is documented and the structure is acknowledged. The reasons the Hirst benchmark matters for the Banksy reading — the increasingly tangible reasons — are the substance of Part 2's corporate forensics and will not be spent here. They are planted as starting markers.
This is the first IP investment, and it is worth being precise about what was bought. Pro-Actiff did not buy art. It did not buy an artist. It bought a publishing rail — a corporate switch-box capable of routing photographic and graphic content from a regional scene into the existing magazine pipeline, under terms that captured the rights upstream of the photographers who took the pictures and the writers who made the marks. The "Banks" placeholder on the Walls on Fire flyer was not yet a person. It was a rights-holding shell with a name attached, waiting for the brand work that would happen over the next five years to fill it in.
A documentary anchor for the operational structure of this rail in its early phase comes from a transactional record dated 2 July 2003, on PYMCA letterhead — Old Street, London EC2A 4HJ, the registered not-for-profit archive's own address — recording the sale of one Weston Super Mare signed edition at a rate of £100, with no edition number recorded on the receipt and the buyer's identifying details handled at the level of customer-record discretion that small-volume scene transactions of the period regularly observed.

The receipt is forensically load-bearing on three counts.
First, it documents that Banksy print sales in mid-2003 were running through PYMCA's not-for-profit archival infrastructure at de minimis transactional volume — single-print sales handled on a generic carbon-copy receipt pad, at hundred-pound price points, eighteen months before Pictures on Walls would incorporate (in 2003 itself, with the operational scaling visible only by 2004) as the standalone retail entity that the legend retroactively credits with handling Banksy's print business from the start. PYMCA, registered as a youth-music-culture archive serving documentary and educational purposes, is not the corporate form authorized to function as a fine-art editions retailer for a commercial print business. The fact that it was functioning as one in mid-2003 is the operational evidence that the publishing rail Pro-Actiff had been incorporated to operate was, at this stage, running its commercial transactions through an adjacent not-for-profit entity rather than through a properly capitalized retail vehicle.
Second, the absence of an edition number on the receipt is a documentation failure that falsifies the retroactive narrative the legend has constructed about Banksy's early-period print editioning. If Banksy editions were being properly numbered, recorded, and tracked at point of sale in 2003 — as the later-published edition catalogues and authentication infrastructure would claim — the edition number would appear on the transactional record. It does not. Which means either the editions were not being numbered at the time of sale and were assigned numbers retroactively when the inventory was reverse-engineered for later authentication, or the sales were occurring outside the editioning system entirely and the editioning system was constructed afterward to absorb them. Both scenarios are inconsistent with the legend's claim of disciplined edition-management from the start.
Third, the chronological math that follows from this transactional pace is foreclosing on the legend's claim that the early POW print editions sold out as announced when the website went live in 2004. If a single-print sale on a carbon-copy receipt at PYMCA in July 2003 represents the operational state of Banksy print commerce eighteen months before the website launch, the inventory would have to have been reverse-engineered to support the late-decade benchmark sales figures the legend now cites. There is no plausible scaling pathway from the documented mid-2003 transactional volume to the late-decade sale records short of the inventory being constructed retroactively, which is the substance Part 2 will address at full evidentiary weight.
For Part 1's purposes, the receipt anchors the broader claim: Pro-Actiff was incorporated as a publishing rail. It used adjacent not-for-profit infrastructure to handle its early commercial transactions. The editioning discipline the legend retrojects onto the early period is not present in the documentary record. The rail was running. It was running on terms that the not-for-profit form was not authorized to support. And the inventory that would later be sold at the prices the late-decade auction record establishes was not, at this point, being properly recorded.
The legend ends at the Walls on Fire flyer. The operational mystery begins at the PYMCA receipt.
VII. The Deck
By the time the venture began its train-start years in 2000 — the period Part 2 will document as the transatlantic two-track development of an iconic logo play and a story-prank-artist-identity brand fashion, with Shepard Fairey advertising Banksy paste-ups on the Los Angeles studio he was renting from Thierry Guetta in his pre-Brainwash real-estate-and-fashion period — the labor pool that had populated the publishing rail since 1996 had produced a documented cast of photographer-artists whose individual biographies any honest forensic reading must hold in the suspect pool until the evidence rules them out.
This is the move the standard Banksy story refuses to make, and the move a reader's fiction-trained brain cannot help making once the cast is presented.

Foremost photographer and photo-editor working the Banksy business through the early and mid-2000s. Departed the operation in 2008. In direct conversation with this author, expressed regret at not having shot the early Supreme work — a comment whose forensic value is that it documents Lazarides's real-time recognition of the cross-sector pattern as it was running.

PYMCA-period scene photographer with Bristol biographical components, including the Bristol Cathedral School and Barton Hill Youth Club elements that would be matched by the Daily Mail's 2008 attribution piece and the Queen Mary University of London geographic-profiling study published in the Journal of Spatial Science in 2016. Whether Gunningham is the technical hand, the face, both, or neither — and the distinction between the face and the technical hand is the operative analytical move — is the substance of Parts 2 and 3.
Maya Hayuk. Photographer and artist out of the Beastie Boys / Grand Royal scene, anchored to the Banksy operation by a mural at Burning Man 2000 and credited in Wall and Piece (Century, 2005). Shepard Fairey. The accountability case the cast cannot be presented without. Fairey's actual late-1990s output, before the OBEY brand was repositioned for the 2008 Obama-era hippie-social-brand pivot, included material moving through the Thrasher and Juxtapoz lanes that read in retrospect as toxic-masculine surf-nazi iconography — work Fairey would prefer to have forgotten in service of the later social-conscience repositioning. The piece names this not to litigate Fairey personally but to hold the cast to the standard the forensic reading requires: real history, judged like everyone else's. Fairey's design-studio production entity — what would later become the in-house operation supporting Thierry Guetta's Mr. Brainwash output, including the liberal incorporation of Banksy graphics as promotional special sauce — is itself a working instance of the design-team production model and a node in the transatlantic infrastructure that will become operationally visible in Part 2.
Hugo Farmer. Dragon Bar bartender in the early 2000s; began as a fabricator on the Banksy operation; ultimately replaced Lazarides as art department construction coordinator. The Farmer trajectory — bar service to fabrication to department head — is the labor-pool argument in single-biography form. He is in the suspect pool for the same reason everyone else is in the suspect pool: he was inside the infrastructure with a camera, with technical skills, and with operational authority across a multi-year period.
The deck is not exhaustive. The Bristol writers — 3D, Inkie, Nick Walker, the Wild Bunch peripheral cohort — are inside the pool. The PYMCA contributing photographers across the 1996–2003 window, numbering in the dozens, are inside the pool. The London art-school graduates who freelanced for Dazed and Sleazenation across the same window, numbering in the hundreds, are inside the pool. The transatlantic photographer-artist network across Bristol, London, New York, Los Angeles, and Berlin in the 1998–2003 window, numbering in the thousands, is the outer pool. Every member of every tier had a camera. Every member of every tier was producing scene documentation that was licensable into the publishing rail Pro-Actiff had been incorporated to operate. Any of them could have been "Banksy" as their side job. Or — the alternative production model the same infrastructure supports — none of them needed to be, because the brand-manager-commissions-the-design-team model (Faile with Lady Aiko and Insect; Fairey's studio absorbing Banksy graphics into Brainwash material; KAWS through Medicom and All Rights Reserved as the transparent inverse-instance) makes the single-artist legend technically unnecessary.
A reader trained by fiction to upload this as a whodunit cannot help uploading it as a whodunit. The piece is not selling it as one. The piece is reporting that the operational structure of the venture, the depth of the labor pool, the global loci of the photo-ops, and the documented production-model alternatives make the whodunit the natural form of the question, which the legend has spent twenty-five years suppressing in favor of the single-artist romance.
VIII. What This Closes and What It Opens
The legend ends here.
It ends because the Walls on Fire flyer of August 1998 documents, in the founders' own signatures, that the principal at the heart of the Bristol scene was operating as Banks DBS — a writer in a crew, not a brand — three months before the corporate vehicle that would incorporate the Banksy brand was filed. It ends because the labor pool the venture would draw from was a documented, transatlantic, hundreds-to-thousands-deep network of photographer-artists already producing the work product the venture needed. It ends because the corporate vehicle itself — Identity Crisis Limited, becoming Pro-Actiff Communications fifty-seven days later, Company Number 03650422 — was named, by its founders, what the founders thought the company did, then renamed for public consumption. It ends because the adjacent-sector instances (Supreme, KAWS, Gorillaz, Hirst's Science Ltd infrastructure) demonstrate that the play Pro-Actiff was running was the standard play of the 1994–2001 IP-aggregation window, executed in the publishing sector against feedstock the publishing sector was best positioned to capture. It ends because the trust-the-promoters convention that the consolidated-media environment of the 1990s had installed in place of the analogue press's verification capacity was sufficient to convert brand-managed and fronted creative product into auction-validated fine art without the documentary friction that would, in any prior decade, have foreclosed the move. It ends, finally, because the PYMCA receipt of July 2003 documents that the publishing rail's commercial transactions were running through not-for-profit archival infrastructure on terms that the not-for-profit form was not authorized to support, with editioning discipline absent at the point of sale, eighteen months before the standalone retail vehicle would incorporate — which means the inventory the legend describes as having sold out from a properly editioned, properly tracked, properly retailed early operation was, as a matter of documentary record, not yet being editioned, tracked, or retailed under any of those forms.
What it opens is the operational mystery, which Parts 2 through 4 will document at full evidentiary weight: the 2000–2003 train-start years, the transatlantic Fairey/Guetta/Los Angeles axis, the 2003 Turf War show and the metal-work pivot that proved objects beat photographs, the 2004 POW incorporation and the formalization of the design-team production model, the 2005 Wall and Piece publication and the manufacture of the artist's-book standard, the 2008 Pest Controlincorporation and the schism that put the technical hand on a work-for-hire contract, the 2019 resignations and the liquidation crash, the 2021 publication of Banksy Explained as the canonization ledger, and the 2026 Ant and Dec v. LilleyHigh Court epilogue that exposed the secret commission and concluded the twenty-year venture inside the British civil court system that had been the venture's silent infrastructure all along.
The face was the next investment.

Glossary
Adjacent-sector tell. A commercial outcome in a parallel industry whose structure is homologous to the case under analysis, used as pattern-recognition evidence rather than direct evidence. Supreme (fashion), Gorillaz (music), and KAWS (fine-art-via-fashion) operate as adjacent-sector tells for the Banksy reading.
Artist's-book standard. The bibliographic threshold required for an artist's published work to qualify as auction-validating fine-art reference material. Achieved by Wall and Piece (2005) through, among other moves, the shift of photography credits from Lazarides to "Banksy."
Attribution anomaly. A documented inconsistency between a fronted creative attribution and observable production evidence. The Noodle character's evolving fashion imprint against Hewlett's documented animation practice is an attribution anomaly. The "Banksy" name's absence from the Walls on Fire announcement is an attribution anomaly.
Brand-manager-commissions-design-team model. The production architecture in which the publicly attributed creator commissions the design and execution of work from a contracted studio or collaborative team, with the resulting product sold under the attributed name. Documented openly in cases such as Faile / Lady Aiko / Insect, the KAWS / Medicom / All Rights Reserved structure, and Mr. Brainwash / Fairey-studio production.
Buffet. The standing transatlantic IP feedstock available for capture in the 1994–2001 window: a forty-year photographic and graphic archive of youth-culture material whose creators had been paid in scene credibility rather than equity.
Convergent inference. An analytical claim supported by the convergence of multiple independent evidentiary chains, none of which is documentary in isolation but whose combined directional weight points to a single conclusion that no alternative principal in the relevant infrastructure could satisfy. The reading of Hack as Pro-Actiff's principal-served is offered at convergent-inference weight.
Criminality-as-feedstock. The commercial thesis that the criminal status of an act authenticates the product derived from it, while the legalization of the venue monetizes the authentication. Documented in the nine-year arc from Operation Anderson prosecutions (1989) to the Walls on Fire municipal mural permit (1998).
Face. The visible public-facing identity within a fronted-name-over-hidden-equity structure. The face is paid for visibility. The face is not the equity holder.
Fronted-name-over-hidden-equity. The corporate structure in which the publicly attributed creator or principal is contractually distinct from the actual rights and equity holders.
Labor pool. The transatlantic cohort of art-school graduates and derelict-scene participants who, in the 1989–2003 window, occupied the gig economy of zine work, scene photography, fabrication, and freelance design that the publishing rail required as its workforce.
Laundering pathway. The documented progression by which street-origin material is moved through gallery, auction, and museum infrastructure to acquire fine-art validation. Established by Basquiat (1982–1988) and Haring (1982–1990); replicated at scale by Saatchi/YBA (1988–1997); applied to the Banksy operation from 2003 forward.
Placeholder identity. A working-title brand name attached to a corporate or operational slot before the human attribution is filled in. "Banks DBS" on the Walls on Fire announcement; "Identity Crisis Limited" at the Pro-Actiff filing.
Publishing rail. The corporate switch-box capable of routing photographic and graphic content from a regional scene into the magazine pipeline under terms that capture rights upstream of the original creators. Pro-Actiff Communications was incorporated as a publishing rail.
Reverse-engineered inventory. The retroactive construction of edition numbering, authentication records, and provenance documentation to support sales records that the original transactional discipline did not establish at the time of sale. Implied as forensic possibility by the editioning absence on the July 2003 PYMCA receipt; substance held for Part 2's full treatment.
Rights-holding shell. A corporate vehicle whose primary asset is contractual rights to attributed creative work, separable from the human attribution itself.
Seed-stage rights vehicle. A corporate entity incorporated at minimal capitalization in a pre-platform window for the purpose of capturing rights to standing inventory before the platform era prices the inventory at scale.
Standing condition. The market and labor environment that exists prior to a venture's incorporation, into which the venture lands as a rational response. The standing condition for Pro-Actiff in 1998 is the substance of Sections I–IV.
Standing inventory. The accumulated analogue archive available for capture at the moment a rights vehicle is incorporated.
Technical hand. The actual producer of the attributed work within a fronted-name-over-hidden-equity structure. Distinct from the face.
Transparent-brand-manager structure. The alternative working model documented in the KAWS operation and parallel cases, in which the principal operates openly as a brand manager and art entrepreneur whose product is character-and-graphic IP, with the gallery and auction systems absorbing brand-managed product as fine art on trust-the-promoters grounds rather than on documentary-attribution grounds.
Trust-the-promoters convention. The working epistemic standard of the consolidated-media environment that succeeded the analogue press's institutional verification capacity. Under this convention, the documentary friction that the analogue press had previously generated against unverified commercial claims — through reportorial density, editorial competition, and the legal infrastructure surrounding print publication — was no longer reliably produced by the consolidated media that replaced it. The convention's commercial effect, inside the 1990s–2010s art-and-fashion brand-architecture window, was to permit the absorption of brand-managed and fronted creative product into fine-art-validation systems on terms that prior decades' verification mechanisms would have refused.
